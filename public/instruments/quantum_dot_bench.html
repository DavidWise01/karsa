// the mechanism

Why size sets the color

In a bulk semiconductor, electrons occupy continuous energy bands separated by a fixed bandgap — the minimum energy to promote an electron and, when it falls back, the energy of the emitted photon. Shrink the crystal below the material's exciton Bohr radius and the electron–hole pair gets physically boxed in. Quantum mechanics says a confined particle's allowed energies rise as the box shrinks — the particle-in-a-box result, where energy scales as 1/L². So a smaller dot has a wider effective gap and emits higher-energy (bluer) light.

Eg(R) = Ebulk + (h² / 8R²)·(1/me + 1/mh) ← confinement term, grows as R shrinks − 1.8·e² / (4πε₀ε·R) ← Coulomb attraction term R = dot radius · me,mh = effective masses · ε = dielectric constant emission wavelength λ ≈ 1239.84 / Eg(eV) nm

This is the Brus equation (L. Brus, 1984) — the effective-mass approximation for a spherical dot. The tuner above evaluates exactly this expression live for the selected material's constants, then converts the resulting photon wavelength to an approximate sRGB color.

Different crystals, different windows

The bulk bandgap sets the baseline; confinement tunes around it. That's why material choice picks the spectral neighborhood and size fine-tunes within it.

Real device dots are usually core–shell (e.g. a CdSe core wrapped in a ZnS shell): the shell passivates surface dangling bonds, raising brightness (quantum yield) and stability. The tuner models the core's confinement only.

// where they show up

From your TV to a qubit

Displays — QLED

A blue backlight excites red- and green-emitting dots tuned to exact wavelengths, producing a wide, pure color gamut. The "QLED" label is literally this physics.

Bio-imaging

Bright, photostable fluorescent tags. Size-tuned emission lets several dot colors be tracked at once under one excitation source.

Photovoltaics

Tunable absorption and multiple-exciton generation make dots candidates for next-gen and tandem solar cells and luminescent concentrators.

Single-photon sources

A single dot can emit one photon at a time — a building block for quantum optics and photonic quantum information.

Spin qubits

Gate-defined dots in silicon or GaAs trap single electrons whose spin encodes a qubit (Loss–DiVincenzo). Real devices use small 2×N dot arrays.

IR detection

Narrow-gap dots (PbS, InAs) sense infrared for night vision, sensing, and telecom-wavelength photodetectors.

// honesty about the model

Where the simple model bends

A teaching tool earns trust by stating its own limits. The Brus effective-mass equation here is the standard first-order picture; here is where reality diverges, so nothing above is mistaken for lab-grade prediction.

  • Very small dots overshoot. Below ~2–2.5 nm the 1/R² term blows up and predicts gaps (and UV wavelengths) higher than measured. Real dots have finite confinement barriers and band non-parabolicity the simple model ignores.
  • Emission ≠ bandgap exactly. Real photoluminescence is red-shifted from the absorption gap by the Stokes shift, and broadened by size dispersion across a real sample. The tuner shows the idealized single-dot gap.
  • Color is approximate. Converting a single wavelength to sRGB is a perceptual approximation; true dot emission is a spectral peak with finite width, not a monochromatic line.
  • Constants are nominal. Effective masses and dielectric constants vary with temperature, strain, and reference source. The values here are representative textbook figures.

Sources behind the model: L. E. Brus, "Electron–electron and electron-hole interactions in small semiconductor crystallites" (J. Chem. Phys., 1984); A. P. Alivisatos, "Semiconductor Clusters, Nanocrystals, and Quantum Dots" (Science, 1996). Wavelength→sRGB via a standard CIE-based approximation.
